Notes: List of Devices

Devices matter because they are choices, not because they are labels. I record each device with enough context to explain what it does in the text.

For each device, record:

  • the name
  • a definition in my own words
  • an example from a text
  • the effect it creates on the reader or audience
DeviceDefinitionExampleEffect
ImageryLanguage that creates a sensory picture.A description of touch, sound, smell, sight, or taste.Makes an abstract idea more immediate and shapes the reader’s response.
JuxtapositionPlacing contrasting ideas or images together.A contrast between physical form and ideology.Makes a difference visible and encourages comparison.
HyperboleDeliberate exaggeration.“All books are really self-help books.”Emphasizes an argument by making the claim difficult to ignore.
PersonificationGiving human qualities to something nonhuman.A book is described as gaining meaning through readers.Presents an object or idea as active and significant.
ParallelismRepeating a grammatical structure.Repeated phrases with the same sentence pattern.Creates rhythm and gives related ideas equal emphasis.

The effect column is provisional until I connect the device to a specific audience, purpose, and context.

Imagery

The class imagery notes divide sensory language into visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, tactile, kinesthetic, and organic imagery. I should not stop after identifying the sense involved. My analysis should move through three steps:

  1. Locate and classify the sensory detail.
  2. Unpack its connotations and the associations it creates.
  3. Connect those associations to the writer’s rhetorical purpose and the reader’s likely response.

For example, descriptions of smoke, laughter, sound, or color in The Great Gatsby do more than make the party visible. They create an atmosphere that can make wealth seem attractive while also exposing its artificiality. The sensory detail gives the larger judgment a concrete emotional form.
